# CME accreditation for ai101.health — research findings

**Researched:** 2026-07-28. All web sources checked that day unless noted.
**Client context:** solo pediatrician, Minnesota, free educational site, no revenue, self-funded.
**Standing rule applied:** every number below carries a source and a date. Where a fee is unpublished, it says so rather than estimating. Vendor-sourced figures are labeled.

---

## Recommendation up front

**Do two things, in this order.**

### 1. Ship the zero-cost option this week (Category 2 framing) — $0

You cannot say the site "qualifies for" or "is eligible for" AMA PRA Category 2 Credit. The AMA's own booklet forbids it in plain language (verbatim quote in Q4 below). What you *can* do is state neutral facts — what the module covers, how long it takes — and let each physician make their own determination. Exact permissible wording is drafted in Q4. This costs nothing, requires no partner, and is genuinely useful to your readers, because Category 2 is real credit that counts toward licensure in many states and toward the AMA PRA itself.

This is not a consolation prize. For a free site with no backend, it is the correct product.

### 2. Get *one scoped module* jointly provided — realistic cost $750–$3,500 one-time

Not the whole site. **Credit equals learner time**, and that is the single biggest cost lever you control. Joint providership is priced per credit hour by nearly every provider with a published schedule (typically $250–$300/credit-hour). A 25-hour "30 Days to Claude Code" would cost $7,500+ in credit fees alone. A tightly scoped 2-credit module — *"Evaluating AI Tools in Clinical Practice"*, say — costs a fraction of that and proves the model.

**Recommended first call: the Minnesota Medical Association.** MMA is the ACCME-recognized accreditor for Minnesota and North Dakota, and its joint providership program explicitly exists "to assist non-accredited organizations **and physicians**" (MMA *Joint Providership Process*, retrieved 2026-07-28). That "and physicians" clause is the finding that makes this viable for you as an individual — most joint providership programs I checked are written for organizations only.

**MMA's fee is not published.** Its process document says: *"If you need a copy of the fee structure please contact the CME Department."* You must call. See open questions.

### The honest cost, and the honest floor

| | Cost |
|---|---|
| Zero-cost path (Category 2 framing) | **$0** |
| Realistic joint-providership floor, 2-credit enduring module | **~$750–$1,600/yr** (benchmarked against published peer schedules) |
| Realistic mid-range, one 2–4 credit module | **~$2,500–$3,500 one-time** |
| MMA specifically | **Unpublished — must call 612-362-3755** |
| Owning MMA accreditation yourself | **$8,500 year one**, then $2,000/yr |
| Owning ACCME accreditation yourself | **$18,900 year one**, then $7,100/yr |

**Is the cheapest real route still too expensive?** No — but only if you scope hard. At full-site scope it absolutely is. A ~$1,000–$3,000 one-time spend on a single flagship module is within reach of one physician funding this personally. A $9,000 spend to accredit thirty days of content is not, and would be a poor use of the money regardless of affordability.

**Infrastructure is not the blocker.** This surprised me and it is good news. You do not need an LMS, user accounts, or a backend. Indiana's schedule states its base fee includes *"Electronic certificate creation and distribution to all participants on list provided to the ISMA by joint provider (List must be provided in either Excel or in a table in Word…)"* — the accredited partner issues certificates from a spreadsheet you hand them. Static site + a hosted form (Tally/Google Forms) + a spreadsheet clears the operational bar. Detail in Q5.

## Q1 — Minnesota Medical Association joint providership

**Does MMA accredit intrastate providers and offer joint providership to non-accredited creators?** Yes to both.

> "The MMA is recognized by the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education to accredit organizations and institutions providing continuing medical education (CME) in Minnesota and North Dakota."
> — [mnmed.org/education-and-events/cme-providers](https://www.mnmed.org/education-and-events/cme-providers), retrieved 2026-07-28

**Individual physicians are explicitly eligible** — this is the key sentence:

> "The purpose of CME Joint Providership is to assist non-accredited organizations **and physicians** in planning and documenting CME activities as well as ensuring compliance with the requirements of the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME)."
> — MMA *Joint Providership Process* (PDF, no version date on document), retrieved 2026-07-28

### Fees — UNPUBLISHED

MMA's *Joint Providership Process* states verbatim:

> "Upon receipt of these items, learner disclosure information and an invoice (to include application and credit fees) will also be created and sent. **If you need a copy of the fee structure please contact the CME Department.**"

So MMA charges (a) an application fee and (b) credit fees, structured like its peers — but the amounts are not on the public site. **This requires a phone call.** Do not budget from the numbers below; they are peer benchmarks only.

What *is* published is MMA's fee schedule for organizations that hold MMA accreditation directly — a different thing, but useful for scale:

| MMA fee (direct accreditation) | Amount |
|---|---|
| Pre-application | $1,500 |
| Initial accreditation (self-study) | $5,000 |
| Reaccreditation (self-study) | $4,000 |
| Annual, 0–50 credits/yr | $2,000 |
| Annual, 50.25–350 credits/yr | $2,000 + $20/credit over 50 |
| Focused resubmission | $2,000 |
| Progress report | $2,500 |

Source: [MMA *2026 Accreditation Fee Schedule*](https://www.mnmed.org/application/files/9517/4189/3008/2026_Fee_Schedule.pdf) (PDF). **Staleness flag:** the document is titled "2026 Accreditation Fee Schedule" but the effective-date line reads "Effective January 1, 2025." That is verbatim from the PDF. Confirm which year is current when you call.

Year one to own MMA accreditation: **$1,500 + $5,000 + $2,000 = $8,500**, then $2,000/yr. Notably cheaper than ACCME (Q2), but it requires being an *organization* with a CME program, a mission, governance, and a self-study — not a realistic target for a one-person website.

### Process and timeline

From the MMA *Joint Providership Process*:

- **Step 1 (≥4 months prior):** contact MMA CME dept; submit Education Plan, preliminary agenda, conflict-of-interest disclosures from planners. Reviewed by an MMA CME rep and a member of CACME (Committee on Accreditation & CME).
- **Step 2 (2–4 months prior):** faculty COI disclosures, draft promotional materials, draft evaluation questions, disclosure form checklist. **Invoice issued at this step.**
- **Step 3 (1–3 months prior):** final promotional materials (MMA must approve *before* distribution), final schedule/syllabus, presentations on request, any ineligible-company letters of agreement.
- **Step 4 (within 60 days after):** summary report, attendance report, final promotional materials, evaluation summary.

**Total lead time: at least 4 months.**

### Terms worth knowing before you commit

From MMA *Joint Providership Guidelines* (marked "Updated 1/1/23" — over three years old, verify currency):

- **Indemnification runs against you.** "The applying organization agrees to indemnify and hold harmless MMA for any claims or expense arising from this educational activity."
- **Fees are nonrefundable** once final activity approval is granted; payable 30 days after approval.
- **MMA controls the marketing.** "Review and approve, in advance, all promotional and onsite materials (brochures, advertisements, flyers, syllabi, **and website information**). The MMA must be listed on all materials as the Accredited Provider." For a live website you edit continuously, this is a real operational constraint — every page change touching the accredited module plausibly needs review. Ask how they handle it.
- **Monitoring at your expense.** "Reserve the right to monitor all educational activities, at the expense of the joint provider. The joint provider will reimburse all travel, lodging and other expenses involved." Should be moot for a web activity; confirm.
- **MMA holds the records** — participant roster and activity file for 6 years. Good: that burden is theirs, not yours.
- **Discretionary.** "The MMA reserves the right to accept or deny joint providership of individual CME activities, at its sole discretion."

**Concern to raise on the call:** the entire process document is written for live events — venue, on-site materials, attendance reports, monitoring travel. It never mentions enduring materials or web-based activities. Whether MMA accepts a self-paced online enduring material at all is the first thing to establish.

### Specialty societies — AAP is closed to you

This is the most consequential negative finding. The AAP *Joint Providership Overview* states:

> "**Only AAP Chapters, Districts and the ABP are eligible for Joint Providership of a CME activity with the Academy.**"
> — [AAP Joint Providership Overview](https://learn.aap.org/pluginfile.php/1/local_public/content/1/COCME/Resources/Joint-Providership.pdf) (PDF), retrieved 2026-07-28

You cannot approach the AAP directly as an individual. **But note the fee it charges those who are eligible:**

> "The joint provider must pay a fee to cover administrative and review costs of an activity. Currently the Joint Providership fee is **$600** with additional fees for presenting a webinar or activity series."

**$600 is far below every other published number in this research.** Which points at a route worth pursuing:

> **Partner with the Minnesota Chapter of the AAP (MNAAP).** MNAAP *is* an AAP Chapter, therefore *is* eligible for $600 joint providership with the Academy. If MNAAP will sponsor and co-plan the activity, you get AAP-designated AMA PRA Category 1 Credit — the most credible possible imprimatur for a pediatric audience — at roughly a tenth of commercial pricing. The AAP is ACCME-accredited, so it can also register the activity for ABP MOC Part 2 (Q3).

Label this honestly: **promising, unverified, and not under your control.** MNAAP would be the joint provider of record; AAP requires involvement "at the very start of the planning process, before the first planning meeting," and you would be contributing content into their process, not running your own. Whether MNAAP has the staff appetite is an open question — but a $600 fee justifies the ask.

AAP timeline: abstract submitted ≥30 days before launch, 30 days for review. Contacts: Virginia Roldan (vroldan@aap.org), Sloane Magee (smagee@aap.org), Katherine McCaskill (kmccaskill@aap.org).

*Separate mechanism, noted for completeness:* "AAP Credit" is available to external organizations — but only those "**accredited to designate** AMA PRA Category 1 Credit(s)™." It layers AAP recognition onto credit you already have the authority to award. Not a path in.

## Q3 — ABP MOC Part 2

### ACCME accreditation is a hard prerequisite. There is no independent path.

> "One way to receive Lifelong Learning (Part 2) points for Maintenance of Certification (MOC) is by participating in continuing medical education (CME) activities **approved by the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME)**."
> — [abp.org/content/moc-credit-cme-activities](https://www.abp.org/content/moc-credit-cme-activities), page states "Last updated on January 29, 2026"

Registration happens in **PARS** (ACCME's Program and Activity Reporting System), which only accredited providers can access. Providers no longer apply to ABP directly for activity approval. So MOC Part 2 rides entirely on top of whatever accreditation arrangement you make — it is not a separate door.

**No program exists for small or individual providers.** I looked; there is none.

### The good news: it is free

> "**Neither ACCME nor any of the participating certifying boards charge CME providers a fee to register activities for MOC.** The ACCME and the certifying boards may revisit their fee structures in the future."
> — [ACCME *CME for Maintenance of Certification Program Guide*](http://accme.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/840_20251110_CME-for-MOC-Program-Guide.pdf), doc 840_20251110, version 3.6 dated 2025-11-10

So once you have a joint provider willing to register it, MOC Part 2 adds **$0** to your cost. Ask for it.

### What ABP specifically requires

From the ABP section of the same guide (pp. 18–19):

- **Credit type:** "ABP allows activities to be registered for Lifelong Learning and Self-Assessment (**Accredited CME with Feedback**)." This is a meaningfully higher bar than plain CME — the activity must evaluate the learner *and give feedback*. Practically: a post-test that shows correct answers with rationales, not just a pass/fail gate.
- **Practice area:** tag it. "General Pediatrics" fits; "Professionalism/Patient Safety/Other Skills" is also on the list and may be the better fit for informatics content.
- **Excluded types:** "CME Providers may not register Committee Learning or Performance Improvement activity types for ABP MOC." Neither applies to you.
- **Learner reporting:** provider submits completion data to ACCME "within 30 days of the learner's completion date and **no later than December 1** of the current calendar year." Miss December 1 and a diplomate may lose the credit for that reporting year.
- **One-per-day rule:** "A learner can only receive participation credit for completing each unique activity (ACCME Activity ID) once per day."
- **Required promotional statement** (verbatim, must appear on materials):
  > "Successful completion of this CME activity, which includes participation in the evaluation component, enables the learner to earn up to [XX] MOC points in the American Board of Pediatrics' (ABP) Maintenance of Certification (MOC) program. It is the CME activity provider's responsibility to submit learner completion information to ACCME for the purpose of granting ABP MOC credit."
- **ABP contact for providers:** support@abpeds.org

**Implication for you:** to earn MOC Part 2 you must collect enough learner identity to match a diplomate (name, email; ABP board ID helps — ABP has a public physician look-up). That is the strongest argument for a real completion form rather than localStorage.

## Q4 — AMA PRA Category 2 (the zero-cost option)

### The controlling text

From [*The AMA Physician's Recognition Award and credit system*, 2017 revision](https://www.ama-assn.org/media/7831/download) (PDF, effective 2017-09-29, doc 17-148701 — this remains the current published booklet as of 2026-07-28), p. 10:

> "AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™ is self-designated and claimed by individual physicians for participation in activities not certified for AMA PRA Category 1 Credit™ that:
> - Comply with the AMA definition of CME; and
> - Comply with the relevant AMA ethical opinions… and
> - Are not promotional; and
> - A physician finds to be a worthwhile learning experience related to his/her practice."

**The restriction — read this carefully:**

> "**Organizations may not certify activities for AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™ or advertise that an activity qualifies for AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™.** Organizations may choose to maintain records of physician participation in activities that have not been certified for AMA PRA Category 1 Credit™ but, since they may not certify or award such credit, should not record them as AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™."

> "A physician must individually assess the educational value for each learning experience in which he or she participates to determine if it is appropriate to claim AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™."

**Note a discrepancy worth knowing about.** The AMA's [web article](https://www.ama-assn.org/education/ama-pra-credit-system/what-know-about-other-kind-cme-credit) (dated 2015-05-20) narrows the same rule to "*Accredited* CME providers must not certify activities for AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™ and may not advertise…" The booklet — the authoritative document, and the later of the two — says "**Organizations**," full stop. **Follow the booklet.** Assume the prohibition binds you.

### Claiming rules (what you may accurately describe *about the system*)

Same page:

- **Documentation:** the physician documents "activity title or description, subject or content area, date(s) of participation and number of credits claimed."
- **Credit calculation:** "60 minutes of participation equal to one (1) AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™; credit is claimed in 15 minute or 0.25 credit increments; physicians must round to the nearest quarter hour."
- **No double-dipping:** "Physicians may not claim AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™ for an activity for which the physician has claimed AMA PRA Category 1 Credit™."
- **Recordkeeping is the physician's:** "Each physician is responsible for claiming and maintaining a record of their AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™."
- Listed qualifying examples include "Participation in activities that have not been certified for AMA PRA Category 1 Credit™," "Reading authoritative medical literature," "Self assessment activities," and "Unstructured online searching and learning."

### Permissible wording for the site

**Do not write:** "Earn Category 2 credit." · "This activity qualifies for X AMA PRA Category 2 Credits." · "Eligible for Category 2." · Anything that issues, certifies, awards, or promises credit. Do not put "Category 2" in a heading, badge, or nav label.

**Safe pattern — state facts, describe the system neutrally, put the determination on the reader:**

> **About CME credit**
>
> This material is not certified for *AMA PRA Category 1 Credit*™.
>
> Under the AMA PRA credit system, physicians may self-designate and self-claim *AMA PRA Category 2 Credit*™ for educational activities they individually determine to be non-promotional, consistent with the AMA definition of CME, and a worthwhile learning experience related to their own practice. That determination is the physician's alone to make, and each physician is responsible for claiming and documenting their own credit. The AMA's requirements are set out in [*The AMA Physician's Recognition Award and credit system*](https://www.ama-assn.org/media/7831/download).
>
> For your own records: this module covers [topic] and takes approximately [N] minutes to complete.
>
> Licensure and credentialing requirements vary. Check with your licensing board about what it accepts.

Why this holds up: it never asserts the activity qualifies, never designates an amount of credit, and attributes the determination to the physician — mirroring the booklet's own framing. Stating your content and your runtime is factual and unavoidable; it is what every website says.

**One further move you may make:** the booklet permits organizations to "maintain records of physician participation" in non-certified activities, provided you do not label them Category 2. So a downloadable completion log — module title, content area, date, minutes spent — is allowed, as long as the document never says "Category 2 Credit" and never states a credit quantity. Give them a time log, not a certificate. Genuinely useful, and free.

**Minnesota licensure caveat, unverified:** whether the Minnesota Board of Medical Practice accepts Category 2 toward its CME requirement, and in what proportion, is outside what I checked. Do not make claims about MN licensure acceptance without confirming with the Board directly.

## Q5 — What the website itself would have to do

Your constraint: static HTML on GitHub Pages, no backend, no accounts, localStorage-only progress. **localStorage cannot verify completion** — it is per-browser, unauthenticated, user-editable, and invisible to you. It is fine as a reading aid and worthless as a compliance record.

### What ACCME actually requires

**Verification of participation — 6 years:**
> "An accredited provider must have mechanisms in place to record and, when authorized by the participating physician, verify participation for **six years** from the date of the CME activity."
> — [ACCME CME Activity and Attendance Records Retention policy](http://accme.org/accreditation-rules/policies/cme-activity-and-attendance-records-retention), retrieved 2026-07-28

**Activity files:** "retain activity files/records of CME activity planning and presentation during the current accreditation term or for the last twelve months, whichever is longer."

**Enduring material accountability:** "Providers are accountable for an enduring material during the period of time it is being offered for CME, and 12 months thereafter."

**Who counts as a learner:** those who "completed all or a portion of the activity and **whose participation can be verified in some manner**." People who merely load the page do not count.

**Credit calculation:** "The credit available for an enduring material activity should indicate the amount of time it takes an individual learner to complete the activity." One credit = 60 minutes. Method: pilot it — have 3–5 physicians time themselves, average, round to the nearest quarter hour, and write down what you did. That memo *is* your methodology documentation.

**Content review:** enduring materials must be reviewed at least every three years, or sooner if the science changes. For AI content this is closer to every six months in practice. PARS entries for enduring material run up to three years; renewing content means entering a **new** activity.

**Assessment of the learner** — from the AMA booklet's core requirements for Category 1: "The CME activity must provide an assessment of the learner that measures achievement of the educational purpose and/or objective of the activity." ACCME does not mandate a post-test specifically, but a post-test is the standard way to satisfy both this and verification at once. For **ABP MOC Part 2 you need assessment *with feedback*** (Q3) — so build the post-test with rationales from the start.

**Disclosure collection:** relevant financial relationships from everyone controlling content — you, plus any reviewer or contributor — collected *before* planning, with conflicts mitigated before the activity. As a solo author with no industry ties this is a short form, but it must exist on paper.

### The minimum you actually have to build

Materially less than you would guess, because **the accredited partner carries the compliance burden**. MMA holds the roster and activity file for 6 years. Indiana's base fee explicitly includes generating and distributing certificates from a list you supply "in either Excel or in a table in Word."

So the minimum viable stack:

1. **A hosted form at the end of the module** — Tally, Google Forms, Jotform. Free tier is fine. Collects: name, email, degree, date completed, post-test answers, evaluation responses. Add ABP board ID if you want MOC.
2. **A post-test with rationales** — 5–10 questions. Can be inside the same form. Shows correct answers on submit; that satisfies "with feedback."
3. **An evaluation section** — did it meet objectives, will it change your practice, perceived commercial bias.
4. **A spreadsheet export** you hand the joint provider. They issue certificates and file the PARS report.
5. **A disclosure statement + accreditation statement** rendered on the page (the joint provider supplies exact required wording).
6. **A dated content-review log** in the repo. You already have git; a `docs/` markdown file with review dates is sufficient and arguably better evidence than most institutions produce.

**No backend. No accounts. No LMS. No database.** Total added cost: $0–$50/month depending on form volume, and the free tiers will likely cover you.

**The one thing you must change:** the accredited module needs a real submit-a-form gate, not a localStorage checkbox. Keep localStorage for the free/uncertified content — it is good UX there.

**Honest caveat:** a self-hosted form means *you* hold learner PII (names, emails, possibly board IDs) outside the accredited provider's systems. Not PHI, so HIPAA is not in play, but decide where it lives and how long you keep it, and say so on the page. Handing it to the joint provider promptly and deleting your copy is the clean answer.

## Q6 — Content eligibility

### The AI-in-medicine content: clearly eligible, with precedent

The AMA definition of CME (HOD policy #300.988), quoted in the booklet and adopted by ACCME:

> "CME consists of educational activities which serve to maintain, develop, or increase the knowledge, skills, and professional performance and relationships that a physician uses to provide services for patients, the public or the profession."

Existing accredited AI CME, all AMA PRA Category 1, all enduring material:
- **AMA Artificial Intelligence Learning Series: AI in Health Care** — 0.50 credits, term 2023-12-12 to 2026-12-12 (listed on ACCME's CME Passport)

- **AMA Ed Hub AI modules** — 7 modules, up to 3.5 credits total
- **Baylor College of Medicine, AI in Healthcare Seminar Series**

No argument needed here. This is settled territory.

### The technical-skills content: eligible, but only under the right framing

**The rule permits it explicitly.** From the AMA booklet, "Educational content of certified CME":

> "**Accredited CME providers may certify nonclinical subjects** (e.g., office management, patient-physician communications, faculty development) for AMA PRA Category 1 Credit™ **as long as these are appropriate to a physician audience and benefit the profession, patient care or public health.**"

ACCME's own examples of qualifying content ([CME Content: Definition and Examples](https://accme.org/rule/cme-content-definition-and-examples/), retrieved 2026-07-28):
- "Management, for physicians responsible for managing a health care facility"
- "Educational methodology, for physicians teaching in a medical school"
- "Practice management, for physicians interested in providing better service to patients"
- "Coding and reimbursement in a medical practice"

**And what fails:**
> Activities addressing "a physician's nonprofessional educational needs or interests, such as **personal financial planning or appreciation of literature or music**, are not CME."

Note the shape of that exclusion: it is about *non-professional* subject matter, not about *non-clinical* subject matter. Coding and reimbursement — as technical and as far from the bedside as git — is explicitly in.

### Precedent for informatics/technical-skills CME: strong

- **AMIA 10x10, "Introduction to Biomedical Informatics and Artificial Intelligence"** — up to **44 AMA PRA Category 1 Credits**, enduring material. Completion "demonstrated by participation in all online sessions and exercises, completion of an independent project presentation, and completion of participant survey."

- AMIA is ACCME-accredited in its own right.

Clinical informatics has been an ABMS subspecialty since 2011. Multi-day accredited courses teaching database concepts, standards, and EHR configuration have existed for over a decade. **Yes, this kind of content gets accredited. Routinely.**

### My honest read on your two technical modules

**"30 Days to Claude Code" — accreditable with reframing.** The gap is not "physicians don't know how to use a CLI." It is: *physicians are adopting AI tools in clinical settings without the ability to evaluate outputs, understand where data goes, or recognize failure modes.* That is a genuine professional practice gap, it is documented in the literature, and the hands-on work is how you close it. Frame the objectives around judgment and safe use, with the tooling as the vehicle. Lead with PHI handling and verification.

**"14 Days to GitHub" — the weaker case. Be honest with yourself here.** Version control taught as version control has no physician practice gap; a reviewer would be right to push back. It becomes defensible only if the objectives are genuinely about physician professional performance — reproducibility and auditability of quality-improvement analyses, safe change management for clinical decision-support content, documenting who changed a protocol and when. If you cannot write the needs assessment without straining, that is your answer. Straining to justify it also puts your joint provider's own accreditation at risk, which they will notice before you do.

**Recommended sequence:** accredit the AI content first. It is unambiguous, it has precedent, and it establishes the relationship. Revisit the technical modules once you have one activity through the process and know what your reviewer actually cares about.

### One eligibility point in your favor

You are **not** an "ineligible company." ACCME's definition: entities "whose primary business is producing, marketing, selling, re-selling, or distributing healthcare products used by or on patients." A pediatric practice delivers clinical services; it does not produce or sell healthcare products. You may therefore control content.

**Two ways that could change** — flagging now because both are cheap to avoid and expensive to unwind:
1. If you ever take sponsorship from an AI vendor, EHR company, or device manufacturer, the Standards for Integrity and Independence engage in full and your joint provider's scrutiny goes up sharply.
2. If ai101.health ever sells a product or becomes a commercial arm of something, revisit the analysis.

Staying free and unsponsored is not just philosophically nice here — it is the thing that keeps this simple and cheap.

## 2. Is $0 achievable, and what exactly may he say?

**Yes — in two distinct senses. Keep them separate, because they permit very different claims.**

### $0 sense one: real Category 1, via piggybacking

Covered above. He may make the full, ordinary claim — because the accredited provider designates the credit and supplies the exact required wording. He says nothing on his own authority. **This is the only $0 route that produces real credit.**

### $0 sense two: a certificate of participation on the site

Permitted, and genuinely useful — but narrower than the phrase "certificate of participation" suggests.

**The controlling text** (this pass located a third AMA source the first pass did not have — the physician-facing FAQ, which is more explicit than the booklet):

> **"20. Does the AMA or accredited CME providers issue certificates for participation in activities that may qualify for AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™?**
> Physicians are not issued certificates which state they have been awarded AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™. This credit is self-claimed and self-documented by physicians. **Organizations may not advertise an activity as meeting the requirements or being eligible for AMA PRA Category 2 Credit™.**"
> — [*AMA PRA Frequently Asked Questions for Physicians*](https://www.ama-assn.org/media/10641/download) (PDF), footer dated **December 2015**, Q20, p. 7

**On the "Organizations" vs "Accredited CME providers" discrepancy the first pass flagged** — I re-checked all three sources on 2026-07-28. The split persists, and it is 2–1 for the strict reading:

| Source | Wording | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| AMA PRA booklet, 2017 revision, p.10 | "**Organizations** may not certify… or advertise…" | Strict |
| AMA Physician FAQ, Dec 2015, Q20 | "**Organizations** may not advertise…" | Strict |
| AMA web article (undated on page) | "**Accredited CME providers** must not certify… and may not advertise…" | Narrow |

**Follow the strict reading.** Two of three say "Organizations," including the booklet, which the AMA's current requirements page still links as the live version — I confirmed on 2026-07-28 that the [2017 revision](https://www.ama-assn.org/media/7831/download) is still the booklet the AMA points to. The first pass's conclusion here was correct and is now better supported.

**So, precisely:**

**He MAY put on a certificate** — these are the exact four elements the AMA instructs physicians to document for their own Category 2 records (booklet p.10; FAQ Q21), so stating them is factual and useful:

- Participant name
- Activity title or description
- Subject / content area
- Date(s) of participation
- Time spent, stated in plain minutes or hours ("This module takes approximately 90 minutes to complete")

**He MAY NOT put on it, or anywhere on the site:**

- The words "AMA PRA Category 2 Credit" — in any heading, badge, nav label, or certificate
- "Qualifies for," "eligible for," "meets the requirements for" any credit
- "Certified," "accredited," "CME credit," "credits awarded"
- Any quantity expressed as *credit* rather than as *time* — "1.5 credits" is out; "approximately 90 minutes" is in

**The distinction that makes this work:** state **time**, never **credit**. Time is a fact about his content. Credit is a designation he has no authority to make. A document headed "Certificate of Participation" listing name, title, content area, date, and minutes is entirely lawful and mentions Category 2 nowhere. The physician, unprompted, does the rest.

The first pass's drafted site wording (Q4) remains sound under this stricter sourcing. **One change I would now make: drop the Category 2 explainer paragraph from the site entirely, or cut it hard.** Minnesota — his home audience — gives Category 2 zero weight (Angle 4), so a long explanation of a credit type that does nothing for most of his readers is wasted words that also walks toward the line the AMA drew. A single sentence pointing physicians to the AMA booklet is enough.

- Self-Assessment activities developed by the ABP and other organizations (found in the ABP Portfolio)
- "CME activities with individual assessment and feedback **that have been approved for MOC credit**" (found via ACCME's CME Passport)
- Life support certifications (ACLS, ATLS, BLS, NRP, PALS, PFCCS)
- Internet searching and learning
- Substance use disorder training

— [ABP, Lifelong Learning (Part 2)](https://www.abp.org/continuing-cert/lifelong-learning-part-2), retrieved 2026-07-28

**There is no propose-your-own-activity route.** Every entry is either an ABP-built activity or an externally built activity that someone else registered.

**The "Internet searching and learning" entry looks like the exception and is not.** That page reads:

> "ABP board-certified pediatricians may earn Lifelong Learning (MOC Part 2) points for credit earned through UpToDate®, as well as any other Internet Searching and Learning activities **that are registered for ABP MOC Part 2 credit**. … Physicians who want to claim ABP MOC Part 2 credit through UpToDate® **must claim it within UpToDate®**."
> — [ABP, Internet Search and Learning (e.g., UpToDate)](https://www.abp.org/continuing-cert/internet-search-and-learning-eg-uptodate), retrieved 2026-07-28

The claiming happens inside an already-registered vendor's system, against credit that vendor issues. It is not open-ended self-study.

**Direct answer to the question as posed: no. A physician cannot complete ai101.health and claim MOC Part 2 individually.** The site would have to be a registered activity, which requires ACCME accreditation, which is the thing we were trying to avoid. This door is closed.

### Angle 2 — ABP Approved Sponsor / Portfolio pathways: **no relief.** Verified negative.

ABP's page for organizations is unambiguous:

> "**MOC Part 2 activities must be submitted through the Accreditation Council of Continuing Education (ACCME) Program and Activity Reporting Service (PARS).**"
> — [ABP, Sponsor Organization Activity Overview](https://www.abp.org/continuing-cert/organizations), retrieved 2026-07-28

"Sponsor Organization" status therefore does **not** bypass ACCME for Part 2 — it is a label applied after an activity is approved, and Part 2 approval still runs through PARS. Sponsor organizations are also defined as "permanent, identifiable entities external to the ABP" — hospitals, health care organizations, networks, national societies, corporations. A one-person website is not the intended shape.

**The Pediatric Portfolio Sponsor Program is Part 4 only, and is not a fit.** It costs **$500** (processing fee, unlimited projects within a two-year period), but it is for quality-improvement projects and is "appropriate for organizations with a well-developed infrastructure for the design, central oversight, and management of QI projects who would have **three or more QI projects** that meet ABP standards in a two-year period." — [ABP, Becoming a Pediatric Portfolio Sponsor](https://www.abp.org/continuing-cert/becoming-pediatric-portfolio-sponsor), retrieved 2026-07-28. Wrong MOC part, wrong scale.

**There is no ABP route for small or individual providers.** The first pass said this; a harder look confirms it.

### Angle 3 — the certificate of participation route: **real, $0, and narrower than it sounds.** See §2 above for the permitted and forbidden wording.

### Angle 4 — state licensure: **this reverses the premise, and it is the most consequential finding of this pass.**

The brief's hypothesis was: if Minnesota pediatricians can satisfy licensure with Category 2, the expensive path buys prestige rather than utility. **The opposite is true.**

**Minnesota is a Category 1-only state.** Verbatim, from the rule itself:

> "**5605.0300 CONTINUING MEDICAL EDUCATION CREDIT.** At least 75 hours of continuing medical education credit must be obtained in any cycle by attendance at continuing medical education activities designated by an accredited sponsor as **Category 1** of the Physician's Recognition Award of the American Medical Association. Continuing medical education sponsors must be accredited by the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME) or by a state medical society recognized by the ACCME as an intrastate accreditor of continuing medical education sponsors.
>
> For purposes of relicensure, the board shall accept the equivalent of Category 1 credit hours as defined by the American Osteopathic Association Bureau of Professional Education, the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, or by organizations that have reciprocal arrangements with the Physician Recognition Award program of the American Medical Association."
> — [Minn. R. 5605.0300](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/rules/5605.0300/), Statutory Authority MS s 147.01, 214.06, 214.12

> "**5605.0100 CONTINUING EDUCATION CYCLES.** During three-year cycles, each physician licensed to practice by this board shall obtain 75 hours of continuing medical education credit as required by this chapter."

**AMA PRA Category 2 Credit appears nowhere in Minnesota's rule and counts for nothing toward Minnesota licensure.** The $0 Category 2 framing is therefore worth **zero licensure value to his primary audience** — Minnesota pediatricians. It retains value only for the AMA PRA award itself and for physicians licensed in states that do count Category 2.

Two riders:

- **The ¶2 "reciprocal arrangements" clause is the opening for AAFP credit** (see §1), and is the single most valuable thing to ask the Board about.
- **Minn. R. 5605.0700 ALTERNATIVE COMPLIANCE:** "The board **may** accept certification or recertification by a member of the American Board of Medical Specialties … **in lieu of** compliance with the continuing education requirements **during the cycle in which certification or recertification is granted.**" Note the two limits — discretionary ("may"), and confined to the cycle in which certification is granted. Do not tell readers that maintaining ABP certification blanket-exempts them from Minnesota CME; the rule does not say that.

**How common is Category 2 acceptance elsewhere?** Mixed, and it does not rescue the argument. Pennsylvania explicitly counts it — the state publishes a [CME Category 2 reporting form](https://www.pa.gov/content/dam/copapwp-pagov/en/dos/department-and-offices/bpoa/medicine/continuing-ed/MedF%20-%20CME%20Category%202%20Reporting%20Form%20-%20Physicians%20Only.pdf) for physicians. California and Alabama are Category 1-only (secondary sources; not individually rule-verified this pass). The AMA's own position is that requirements "vary depending on the jurisdiction" and physicians "should contact the appropriate state medical board directly."

**Bottom line for Angle 4: Category 1 has real, checkable utility for his Minnesota audience. Category 2 does not.** That strengthens, not weakens, the case for eventually buying accredited credit — and it means the free Category 2 framing should be presented modestly rather than as the headline.

### Angle 6 — fee waivers and subsidies: **no published program exists, but every fee is negotiable, and I can prove it.**

The structural fact worth knowing:

> The ACCME "does not impose any financial requirements or fees for engaging in joint providership," and maintains **no policy that requires or precludes** accredited providers from charging a joint providership fee.
> — ACCME joint providership guidance, retrieved 2026-07-28

**Every joint providership fee in this entire document is set unilaterally by the partner, not mandated by any accreditor.** There is no rate card he is arguing against. That means asking for a reduction is a normal commercial conversation, not a request for an exception.

**Documented waiver precedent:** Carilion Clinic waives administrative fees for its affiliates (P&P revision February 2025, quoted in §1). That is a written, published waiver — proof the category exists.

**What I did not find, having asked the question directly:** no accreditor, state society, or health system publishes a waiver or reduced rate for *free, non-commercial, public-benefit* education as such. If it exists it is unadvertised and discretionary. **The right ask, verbatim, is: "This is a free, unsponsored, non-commercial educational activity with no industry support and no revenue. Do you have a reduced or waived rate for that?"** The absence of industry support is his strongest lever — it removes the commercial-support review burden that drives 25% surcharges elsewhere (ISMA adds 25% for industry-supported activities).

### Angle 7 — piggybacking: **the strongest finding of this pass.** Covered in §1.

Beyond MNAAP, Essentia, and hospital grand rounds, two further vehicles worth one email each: the **University of Minnesota / Children's Minnesota pediatric residency curricula** (a lecture inside an ACGME/ACCME-covered series costs him nothing), and **existing accredited podcasts or journal clubs** in the pediatric and informatics space.

One item purely in his own favour, unrelated to his audience: the AMA raised faculty credit for original presentations. Physicians "may now claim **up to four credits per each hour of presentation**" for eligible original presentations at live Category 1 activities on or after Jan 1, 2023 — up from the old 2:1 ratio ([AMA PRA credit system requirements](https://www.ama-assn.org/education/ama-pra-credit-system/ama-pra-credit-system-requirements), retrieved 2026-07-28). **Teaching this material at an accredited activity earns him up to 4 Category 1 credits per presentation hour, at no cost.**

### Angle 8 — what does credit actually buy him? An honest answer.

**If the goal is reach and credibility: credit buys less than it costs.** Nobody discovers a website because it offers CME. Accredited credit adds a compliance apparatus — post-tests, verification of participation, six-year record retention, three-year content review, disclosure collection, and in MMA's case advance approval of website copy — to a site whose entire advantage is that he can update it the day a model ships. For AI content that goes stale in months, an accredited enduring activity is a recurring obligation, not a one-time purchase.

**If the goal is helping physicians meet a real requirement: credit buys quite a lot — but only Category 1, and only in the states that matter.** Minnesota requires 75 Category 1 hours per three-year cycle and accepts nothing self-designated. That is a genuine need his content could serve.

**The sequencing this implies:**

1. **Now, $0:** ship the completion certificate (time, not credit) and stop there on the site itself. Do not build a Category 2 explainer of any length.
2. **Now, $0:** propose one session to MNAAP and make one call to Essentia. Both give real Category 1, cost nothing, and — more valuable than the credit — tell him whether physicians actually want this content in accredited form before he spends anything.
3. **Only if step 2 shows demand:** buy one scoped module. AAFP at $625 + $1,195 if he wants it on his own site under his own control; a Minnesota accredited partner if AMA PRA Category 1 and ABP MOC matter more than autonomy.

**The thing worth spending money on is not the credit. It is finding out whether anyone wants it.** Step 2 answers that for free.

## 4. Is $600 the floor for accredited Category 1? — plain answer

**No, in both directions, and the $600 figure should stop being treated as a benchmark.**

**$600 is not a price he can buy.** It is the fee the **AAP charges a chapter** for joint providership with the Academy. He is not a chapter and cannot pay it. It only reaches him if MNAAP chooses to sponsor him *and* uses the AAP route — and MNAAP's own accreditation statement points at the **MMA** instead. So the $600 may not even be the operative number in the one scenario the first pass built it on.

**There is something cheaper, and it is $0.** Delivering the content inside an existing accredited activity — MNAAP, Essentia, or a hospital where he has privileges — produces real AMA PRA Category 1 Credit at no cost to him. That is a genuine floor, not a technicality. Its limitation is scope, not legitimacy: the credit attaches to the session, not the website.

**For credit attached to ai101.health itself, the floor is not established, because the cheapest candidates will not publish.** What can be stated with sources:

| Route | Real cost | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Piggyback (MNAAP / Essentia / hospital affiliate) | **$0** | Mechanism verified; his access unverified |
| ISMA, 1-credit internet enduring | **$450 yr 1** ($300 + $150) | Fee published but 3 yrs old; out-of-state eligibility unverified |
| AAFP, ≤5-credit enduring | **$625** (+$1,195 one-time) | **Fees verified and current**; not Category 1; no ABP MOC |
| MMA, Essentia, MNAAP, UMN OCPD | **Unpublished** | Must call |

**So: is the cheapest real route still too expensive? No — and it is cheaper than the first pass concluded.** The first pass's answer of "~$600 floor, realistically $750–$3,500" was too pessimistic on two counts: it missed the AAFP pathway entirely, and it treated piggybacking as out of scope when it is the cheapest genuine route to Category 1 that exists.

**What I did not find, and want to be plain about:** no accreditor waives fees for free public-benefit education as a published program; no ABP route lets a physician self-claim MOC for completing his site; and nothing lets a non-accredited website designate AMA PRA Category 1 Credit. The ACCME/state-society gate is absolute — AMA states it directly, AAFP restates it against its own product. Any offer that appears to get around it is not what it claims to be.
