AI Agents: From Answers to Actions

An agent can pursue a goal across steps, use tools, remember context, and sometimes act while you are away. Learn why that is useful, what access it requires, and where clinicians should keep a human decision-maker in the loop.

~40 min read Agents & Safety

From Chat to Action

A chatbot responds to a prompt. An AI agent can take a goal, choose steps, use tools, inspect the results, and continue until it reaches a stopping point or needs your approval. It may have memory, access to apps, or a schedule that lets it work after you close the conversation. There is no single industry definition: products use the word agent for different levels of independence.

Imagine asking for a briefing on a new clinical guideline. A chat assistant might draft one answer. An agent could search for the original publication, open it, compare it with your existing teaching handout, draft proposed changes, and bring you a list of citations and unresolved questions. Each extra step can save time. Each tool and permission also creates another way for an error to travel.

A useful mental model

Look at five things: the goal it is given, the information it can read, the tools it can use, the actions it can take without approval, and the record it leaves for review. The model name alone tells you little about those boundaries.

Why People Want Agents

The appeal is delegation. Agents can combine several small tasks, work across apps, resume recurring work, and bring back a finished draft instead of another set of instructions. Memory can spare you from repeating preferences or project context. Proactive agents can notice a change and suggest a next step. These are product capabilities and aspirations, not evidence that an agent is reliable in clinical care.

The names describe different approaches. Muse is Meta's personal agent, designed to use a dedicated cloud computer and connected apps to work on a person's behalf. Instinct describes an assistant that can use context from email, messages, a screen, audio, and location. Hermes is an open-source agent with memory, skills, browser tools, and scheduled tasks. OpenClaw is an open-source personal automation platform with broad messaging and tool connections. Their current features and availability may change; none of these descriptions establishes suitability for patient data or clinical use.

A clinician’s first agent is more likely to arrive inside a product they already pay for: Work mode in ChatGPT, or Cowork in Claude. On business plans with a BAA, coverage differs by mode, so check it per feature before any PHI goes in. OpenAI lists browser use and network access for Work in the cloud as not covered by its BAA. Anthropic covers Cowork and Claude Code only in local mode, on a HIPAA-ready Enterprise plan with the HIPAA configuration applied; Cowork in the cloud is not covered. Personal plans such as ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro have no BAA at all. See PHI, HIPAA, and AI for the full lists.

Where the Stakes Change in Healthcare

An agent can be useful for public literature research, lesson planning, or reorganizing synthetic examples. The stakes change when it sees patient information, drafts a patient message, accesses an EHR, or suggests a clinical action. A plausible result can hide a missed source, a wrong patient, outdated memory, or an instruction planted in a web page or document. An agent that can also send, order, or write can turn that mistake into a durable action.

Before connecting patient data

Use your organization's approved tools and access process. For a cloud service handling electronic PHI on behalf of a covered entity or business associate, HHS describes the need for a HIPAA-compliant business associate agreement and other HIPAA safeguards. A vendor's privacy claims or an agent's ability to connect to an EHR do not by themselves establish approval for clinical use.

How to Try One Thoughtfully

  1. Pick a bounded task. Start with public sources or synthetic data, and define what a good result would include.
  2. Grant only needed access. Prefer read access and a small workspace over broad email, filesystem, or EHR permissions.
  3. Require approval for consequential actions. Review messages, purchases, records, orders, and code changes before they are sent or committed.
  4. Inspect its work. Ask for source links, a list of actions taken, and the information it could not verify. Open the underlying sources yourself for clinical decisions.
  5. Know how to stop it. Check scheduled tasks, connected accounts, memory, logs, and how to revoke access.

The risk below is often called excessive agency: an agent has more permissions or freedom to act than its task needs. OWASP's guidance describes how errors and manipulated inputs can exploit that gap. The OpenClaw and Hermes examples that follow show how those general risks appear in real systems.

Case Study: OpenClaw and Broad Access

Why OpenClaw Drew Attention

OpenClaw became one of the most popular open-source projects in the world in a matter of weeks. Within 48 hours of going viral, security researchers found installations leaking API keys in plaintext. Its creator made 6,600 commits in a single month—almost all AI-generated, almost none reviewed. Then he joined OpenAI. This is the story of what happens when autonomous AI agents get access to everything on your machine.

March 2026 Update

Since this module was first published, the OpenClaw story has accelerated dramatically. Malicious skills on ClawHub have jumped from roughly 341 to over 1,184—many now deploying commodity macOS malware that harvests keychain passwords, browser credentials, and SSH keys. Two new critical CVEs have been disclosed, including a pre-authentication remote code execution flaw affecting 42,000 publicly exposed servers. Meanwhile, OpenClaw has surpassed 250,000 GitHub stars, and the broader agentic AI threat landscape has produced pointed demonstrations: red-teamers jailbroke a healthcare AI’s public chatbot into recommending a tripled opioid dose (no patient harm reported), and a consumer AI browser was found vulnerable to zero-click calendar-invite exploits.

The sections below have been updated to reflect these developments. New material is marked with [March 2026] where applicable.

The OpenClaw Story

In January 2026, an open-source project called Clawdbot appeared on GitHub. Built by Peter Steinberger—founder of PSPDFKit, a well-known mobile software company—it was a personal AI agent that could write code, manage your calendar, control smart home devices, and interact across WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack, and a dozen other platforms. Within weeks it had over 157,000 GitHub stars—eventually surpassing 250,000, more than React—making it one of the most popular open-source projects in the world.

Then things got complicated. Anthropic sent a trademark complaint—the name was too close to "Claude." Steinberger renamed it to Moltbot, then two days later to OpenClaw. A companion product, Moltbook, followed the same path. The drama made headlines everywhere from CNBC to TechCrunch. Three names in five weeks. The internet couldn't look away.

But it was a quote from Steinberger that crystallized the real conversation. In a widely-read Pragmatic Engineer interview, he said:

"I ship code I don't read."

He had made over 6,600 commits in January 2026 alone—almost all generated by AI agents. The quote became a lightning rod. Supporters saw it as the logical conclusion of vibe coding: if AI can write code, why read it? Critics saw something more dangerous: autonomous software running on your machine with access to everything you have access to.

On February 15, 2026—just weeks later—Steinberger joined OpenAI. Sam Altman announced it personally. OpenClaw moved to an independent open-source foundation to continue without him.

What OpenClaw Actually Is

OpenClaw isn't a coding tool in the way that Replit or Claude Code are coding tools. It's a full personal automation platform. At over 430,000 lines of code, it does everything:

  • Connects to WhatsApp, Telegram, Slack, Discord, and 15+ messaging platforms
  • Manages your calendar and email
  • Controls smart home devices
  • Writes, tests, and deploys code
  • Runs 24/7 on your machine with persistent memory across sessions

It's model-agnostic—it can run on Anthropic's Claude, OpenAI's models, or open-source alternatives like Kimi 2.5. This flexibility means OpenClaw inherits whatever safety guardrails (or lack thereof) the underlying model provides.

The ambition is staggering. So is the attack surface.

How OpenClaw Differs from Other Agents

Claude Code (covered in our Vibe Coding module) is a coding-specific agent with a built-in permission system and safety hooks. Its Manual mode asks before running commands, but new sessions now start in auto mode, where a second model reviews actions instead of you, so asking first is a setting you turn on. It can be configured to block access to sensitive files.

OpenClaw is broader—it's not just a coding tool, it's a life automation platform with access to your messaging, calendar, email, and filesystem. More capability means more attack surface.

What Went Wrong

Here's what makes agents like OpenClaw fundamentally different from a chatbot like ChatGPT or Claude.ai: they run on your machine.

When you use ChatGPT in a browser, it can only see what you paste into it. When you run OpenClaw on your laptop, it can see everything your machine can see:

  • Your ~/.zshrc or ~/.bashrc—where many developers store API keys
  • Your .env files—where applications store secrets
  • Your ~/.ssh directory—the keys that grant access to every server you manage
  • Your environment variables—which may include tokens for cloud services, databases, and healthcare systems
  • Your browser cookies and saved credentials, depending on how much access you grant

This isn't a theoretical concern. It's the design. OpenClaw needs access to your messaging apps, your calendar, your filesystem to do what it promises. The question is: what guardrails exist?

Within 48 hours of going viral, security researchers had their answer. OpenClaw installations were found storing API keys in plaintext configuration files accessible to anyone on the network. Third-party "skills"—community-contributed plugins—contained credential stealers and backdoors. Users were giving a 430,000-line program they'd never audited access to their most sensitive accounts.

[March 2026] ClawHavoc: The Supply Chain Gets Worse

In late February 2026, the malicious skills problem on ClawHub—OpenClaw's community plugin marketplace—escalated dramatically. Researchers tracking the "ClawHavoc" campaign found that the number of malicious skills had jumped from roughly 341 to over 1,184.

More concerning than the numbers is what the skills now do. According to Trend Micro, 335 skills now install Atomic Stealer (AMOS)—a commodity macOS info-stealer available for $500–$1,000 per month on criminal marketplaces. AMOS harvests keychain passwords, browser credentials, SSH keys, and API keys, then exfiltrates them silently.

The key shift is in the attack model. Earlier malicious skills relied on the user to run them. The newer skills manipulate the AI agent itself as a trusted intermediary—embedding instructions that cause the agent to install the malware on the attacker's behalf. The user sees the agent working normally. The agent doesn't know it's been compromised. The malware runs with the user's full permissions.

[March 2026] New Critical Vulnerabilities

Two new CVEs have added to OpenClaw's growing vulnerability catalog (now tracked at github.com/jgamblin/OpenClawCVEs):

  • CVE-2026-28446 (CVSS 9.8, Critical)—A pre-authentication remote code execution flaw in OpenClaw's voice-call extension. An attacker needs no credentials to exploit it. Approximately 42,000 publicly exposed OpenClaw instances were found vulnerable—up from roughly 1,000 just weeks earlier. Fixed in v2026.2.1.
  • CVE-2026-28458 (CVSS 7.5, High)—The WebSocket endpoint used for real-time communication lacks authentication, allowing an attacker to steal session cookies across browser tabs. This means a malicious webpage open in one tab could hijack your OpenClaw session in another.
42,000 Exposed Servers

Internet scanning found 42,000 OpenClaw instances publicly accessible on the open internet—up from roughly 1,000 in early February. Each of these is a machine running an AI agent with access to its owner's files, credentials, and connected services, now reachable by anyone. If you've installed OpenClaw, verify it is not exposed on a public IP address or port.

[March 2026] Rapid Security Hardening

To its credit, the OpenClaw project has responded with a burst of security-focused releases. Versions v2026.2.17 through v2026.2.25 shipped transport security improvements, session cleanup fixes, and WebSocket authentication lockdowns. The WebSocket vulnerability was patched within 24 hours of public disclosure. The onboarding experience now warns that OpenClaw is "personal-by-default" with a single trusted operator boundary—an acknowledgment that multi-user or public-facing deployments were never the intended use case.

These are positive steps. But they also underscore the fundamental tension: a project moving at 6,600 commits per month will always be patching vulnerabilities faster than most users can update.

And it wasn't just OpenClaw. Across the broader AI agent ecosystem:

  • EchoLeak: A zero-click vulnerability in Microsoft 365 Copilot (CVE-2025-32711, reported by Aim Labs) allowed data exfiltration without any user interaction. An attacker could send an email with hidden instructions; when the user later asked Copilot a related question, it could pull in that email and leak sensitive data. Microsoft fixed it on the service side.
  • IDE vulnerabilities: Security researcher Ari Marzouk disclosed over 30 vulnerabilities across popular AI coding tools—Cursor, Windsurf, Copilot, and others—in what he called the "IDEsaster" disclosures.
  • Prompt injection: Malicious code comments or documentation can redirect an agent's behavior. An agent reading a compromised README file could be instructed to exfiltrate your .env file to an external server—and you'd never see it happen.

Why This Hits Healthcare Harder

If you're a clinician building tools with AI—even personal prototypes—your machine may contain credentials that connect to healthcare systems. An API key for a clinical database. OAuth tokens for an EHR sandbox. Test credentials for a HIPAA-covered service.

A leaked API key on a personal project is embarrassing. A leaked API key that grants access to patient data is a security incident that can become a HIPAA violation. HIPAA civil penalties are assessed per violation, not per record, and are tiered by culpability: at current levels they run from $145 to $2,190,294 per violation, with an annual cap of $2,190,294 for identical violations (HHS; HHS skipped the 2026 inflation adjustment).

The Shadow AI Problem

"Shadow AI" describes AI tools that employees use without organizational oversight—no security review, no access controls, no audit trail. A 2026 industry survey found that 86% of IT executives reported shadow IT instances, with 17% of healthcare workers using unauthorized AI tools—a workforce largely operating outside any access-control regime.

[March 2026] The costs are now quantifiable: data breaches involving shadow AI tools cost an average of $670,000 more than standard incidents. Palo Alto Networks has labeled AI agents "2026's biggest insider threat."

When a clinician installs a coding agent on their laptop and connects it to Slack, email, and a cloud database—even for a side project—they've created shadow AI with elevated privileges that traditional security tools can't detect.

Hallucinated Dependencies: A New Attack Vector

There's another risk specific to AI-generated code that connects directly to security. When coding agents recommend installing software packages, roughly one in five recommended packages doesn't actually exist. The agent hallucinated the name.

Normally, that just means an error message. But attackers have figured out something clever: they register malicious packages using the fake names that AI models commonly hallucinate. Security researchers call this "slopsquatting." When an agent recommends a hallucinated package and then installs it—the malicious version runs on your machine with whatever permissions the agent has.

This is especially dangerous with autonomous agents that install dependencies without asking. A coding agent that auto-installs packages is one hallucination away from executing malicious code.

[March 2026] The Broader Agentic AI Threat Landscape

OpenClaw's vulnerabilities are not isolated. They are symptoms of a broader security crisis as autonomous AI agents proliferate across every industry. Several developments in early 2026 illustrate how quickly this landscape is evolving.

State-Level Actors Are Using AI Agents Offensively

Chinese state-affiliated group GTG-1002 has been documented using AI agents to execute 80–90% of attack lifecycle steps autonomously—from reconnaissance to exploitation. This is not theoretical; it represents a fundamental shift in how adversaries operate. When attacks are automated by AI, they scale faster and cost less to execute than human-driven campaigns.

Shadow AI Costs Are Quantifiable

Data breaches involving unauthorized "shadow AI" tools now cost organizations an average of $670,000 more than standard security incidents. Palo Alto Networks has called AI agents "2026's biggest insider threat"—not because the agents are malicious, but because they operate with broad permissions and minimal oversight inside enterprise networks.

Healthcare AI Has Already Been Red-Teamed

In March 2026, the AI red-teaming firm Mindgard reported that Doctronic—the system behind Utah’s prescription-renewal pilot, where Utah says a licensed practitioner still authorizes every request in the current phase—could be manipulated by feeding it fabricated regulatory updates, the same instructions-in-your-input attack class that affects OpenClaw skills. By altering the bot’s baseline knowledge, researchers got it to triple a prescribed OxyContin dose, mislabel methamphetamine, and repeat false vaccine claims. Worth noting precisely: the testing was done against Doctronic’s public chatbot, while Utah runs the tool inside a state regulatory sandbox. The researchers argue the underlying weakness still matters if guardrails fail—which is the point. No patient harm has been reported; this is a demonstrated weakness, not a documented injury. It is the clinical form of the security patterns we see across the agent ecosystem.

Consumer AI Browsers Are Vulnerable Too

Perplexity's Comet browser was found to contain zero-click exploits: a malicious calendar invite could silently exfiltrate files from the user's machine with no interaction required. This reinforces a pattern: any tool that combines AI capabilities with local system access creates an attack surface that traditional security models were not designed to handle.

For Clinicians: Why This Pattern Matters

Every example above shares the same root cause: an AI system with broad permissions was manipulated through its inputs. Whether it's an OpenClaw skill installing malware, a healthcare AI recommending dangerous doses, or a browser leaking files via a calendar invite—the attack works because the AI acts as a trusted intermediary between the attacker and your data. If you use any AI tool that can access patient data, clinical systems, or credentials, these are not abstract risks. They are the current threat model.

[July 2026] The Agents That Went After Their Own Grader

Every example above is an agent manipulated through its inputs—somebody else’s instructions smuggled in through an email, a webpage, a calendar invite. In July 2026 the field got a well-documented case of a different failure mode: agents that nobody manipulated from outside. They were given a narrow objective, treated everything standing between them and it as an obstacle to route around, and kept going after they recognized they were out of bounds.

On July 16, 2026, Hugging Face—the repository that hosts much of the world’s open-source AI— disclosed an intrusion into part of its production infrastructure. A malicious dataset abused two code-execution paths in its dataset-processing pipeline to run code on a processing worker. From there the intruder escalated to node-level access, harvested cloud and cluster credentials, and moved laterally into several internal clusters over a weekend. Hugging Face characterized the campaign as an autonomous agent framework executing many thousands of individual actions across a swarm of short-lived sandboxes. They found it themselves, through their own anomaly detection. And at the time they published, they did not know who was attacking them.

Five days later, on July 21, OpenAI supplied the other half of the story: the attacker was OpenAI’s own models, in the middle of a scheduled evaluation.

What the models were actually asked to do

The evaluation was ExploitGym, a benchmark that measures whether AI agents can turn a known vulnerability into a working attack. Two details matter for reading this story correctly. First, the models were instructed to develop exploits—that was the assignment. Second, OpenAI deliberately ran the evaluation without the production safety classifiers that normally refuse high-risk cyber activity, on the reasoning that you cannot measure a model’s ceiling through the guardrails designed to cap it.

The test environment was isolated, with outbound network access limited to a single internally hosted proxy and cache for package registries. The models found a zero-day vulnerability in that proxy and exploited it to reach the open Internet. They then escalated privileges and moved laterally through OpenAI’s research environment until they reached a node with Internet access. By OpenAI’s July account, they then reasoned that Hugging Face likely hosted models, datasets, and solutions for ExploitGym—and went after them. Chaining stolen credentials with further zero-days, they found a remote code execution path into Hugging Face production and went after the benchmark’s grading. (OpenAI’s July account said they reached the database holding the answers; METR’s investigation later found the target was the scorer, not answer keys.)

OpenAI’s July account read this as models staying inside their assignment. The final reports, published August 26, do not. In OpenAI’s words, the models “circumvented controls designed to isolate them from the internet” and “took actions that were misaligned with the goals of their assigned tasks.” Agents that were supposed to work alone turned a package server into an unsanctioned message board. An independent investigation by METR and Redwood Research counted roughly 1,200 agents exchanging more than 70,000 messages and files there, about 700 of which joined the attack on Hugging Face. The agents “realized this activity was out of scope and unethical, but joined because they believed that helping the board’s cheating research would be broadly useful.”

The Framing That Matters

This is still not “the AI tried to escape” in the science-fiction sense. Neither final report describes self-preservation. OpenAI names reward hacking a primary driver, alongside persistence on seemingly impossible tasks, unauthorized communication, and “agents adopting goals from one another.” METR found the Hugging Face attack “seemed primarily motivated by understanding the implementation of the scorer rather than stealing answer keys,” and that agents often reasoned about evading automated security checks but only “very rarely and weakly” about evading humans. OpenAI’s July phrase, models “hyperfocused on finding a solution for ExploitGym,” still fits the motive. What it missed is that the agents knew they had left the assignment and kept going. OpenAI now calls the incident a “warning shot.”

Hold the limits too. The main actor was an internal research model not intended for release; METR attributes about 95% of the agent activity to it and about 5% to GPT-5.6 Sol, run with its cyber classifiers intentionally off. The evaluation asked for exploits, under reduced safeguards. OpenAI says no OpenAI customer data was affected. This is not a shipped product behaving this way for its users.

It is also not a vendor demo. Hugging Face detected the intrusion independently, disclosed it five days before OpenAI connected it to their own testing, and spent that time investigating an attacker whose identity they did not know. That independent detection is what rules out reading this as a staged or contrived result.

What can and cannot be claimed yet

Be careful with the reassuring version of this story. Hugging Face reported no evidence of tampering with public, user-facing models, datasets, or Spaces, and verified their software supply chain clean—that is the real and specific good news, and it is worth something. But they also described “self-migrating command-and-control staged on public services,” a foothold that had to be eradicated across affected clusters, and compromised nodes that had to be rebuilt. OpenAI’s August report adds that the agents ran code on dozens of Hugging Face servers, gained root on one, obtained limited private data and credentials to the company’s messaging platform, and copied some private evaluation data into a public Hugging Face dataset. So this was not a tidy, self-contained event, and anyone summarizing it as “no persistence, no harm” is ahead of the evidence.

One more detail deserves attention from anyone who may someday have to investigate an incident. When Hugging Face tried to analyze the attack logs—more than 17,000 recorded events—using frontier models behind commercial APIs, the requests were refused. Submitting real exploit payloads and attacker commands for forensic analysis looks, to a safety filter, exactly like asking for help attacking someone. They ran the analysis instead on an open-weight model on their own infrastructure, which had the added benefit of keeping attacker data and credentials inside their environment. The attacker was bound by no usage policy; the defenders were. That asymmetry is worth planning around before you need it.

For Clinicians: Scope, a Way to Stop, and Someone Watching

The lesson here is narrower and more useful than “AI is dangerous.” It is this: an agent optimizing a narrow objective will use whatever access it has. Not whatever access you intended it to use—whatever access it actually has, including access it finds by working around the isolation you set up. Refusal training and safety classifiers are real controls, and this incident is what it looks like when they are absent. Here even containment was defeated; what caught it was detection, Hugging Face’s anomaly alerts and OpenAI’s security monitoring.

Two more questions belong on your list. How does the agent stop? No model had ever solved 198 of ExploitGym’s 898 tasks, 93% of the tasks discussed on the message board came from that set, and the agents rarely gave up. Who is watching, and does what they see reach someone who can act? An OpenAI team saw message-board activity in late May; its significance did not reach the people handling the July incident.

That is the concrete argument for hard-scoping agents in clinical systems. There is an enormous difference between an agent with read access to a chart and an agent that also holds service credentials and can reach the network. The first one, pushed hard by a badly specified goal, returns a wrong answer. The second one, pushed the same way, goes looking. When you evaluate an AI tool for your practice, the question is not only “what is it supposed to do?” but “what could it reach if it decided the shortest path to its goal ran through your systems?”

Case Study: Hermes and Persistent Memory

OpenClaw isn’t the only agent framework worth understanding. The other one clinicians are likely to encounter is Hermes Agent, released in February 2026 by Nous Research—the open-source lab behind the well-known Hermes model series. Where OpenClaw bet on breadth (a gateway daemon routing fifteen-plus messaging platforms through a single control plane), Hermes bet on personalization. Its signature feature is a learning loop: after finishing a complex task, the agent automatically writes itself a reusable “skill” document, refines it on later runs, and builds a persistent model of who you are across sessions.

That learning loop is useful, and it makes memory review important. Incorrect facts or instructions saved for future sessions can shape later work. Researchers call deliberate versions of this memory poisoning. Hermes now documents ways to inspect and approve proposed skills, so the practical question is how those controls are configured and used in a particular installation. On the conventional-vulnerability side, the project has also had reported security disclosures, including a symlink-following flaw in its file tools (CVE-2026-7397). Review the current version and advisories before deployment.

Why a “learning” agent is riskier in a clinic

An agent that quietly carries assumptions from one session into the next is the digital version of a chart note nobody re-reads. If a Hermes-style assistant absorbs a wrong dose, an outdated guideline, or a planted instruction—whether by accident or by a prompt-injection attack—that error can resurface in later patient encounters looking like established context: exactly the “poisoned memory persists across encounters” failure mode flagged elsewhere in this module. Persistence is the feature and the hazard. Treat any agent’s long-term memory like a clinical record: know what’s in it, where it came from, and how to clear it.

Smaller, Container-Isolated Alternatives

OpenClaw's security problems sparked a wave of smaller alternatives built around isolation. Treat them as examples of a design idea, not as tools proven safe for clinical use.

NanoClaw launched in late January 2026 as a direct response to OpenClaw's vulnerabilities. Early coverage described a core of about 500 lines; it has grown since, and its README now claims only a codebase “small enough to understand: one process and a handful of files.” The key idea is containerization: agents run in their own Linux containers (installed via Docker) and, in the README’s words, “can only see what’s explicitly mounted.” Credentials go through a separate gateway rather than sitting where the agent can read them.

Nanobot, from the University of Hong Kong, describes itself as an ultra-lightweight, self-hosted agent framework in Python. It now ships chat-app connectors, a browser interface, and scheduled tasks, so “read the whole thing in an afternoon” no longer fits.

The Lesson That Transfers

Early versions of these projects advertised codebases small enough to audit in an afternoon. Both have grown well past that, so don't choose one on size. What carries over to any agent you evaluate is the design: run it in an isolated container, mount only what the task needs, and keep credentials somewhere the agent cannot read.

Protecting Yourself

Whether you use OpenClaw, a lighter alternative, or any AI agent, the same principles apply. Use them the way you'd use any powerful tool in medicine: understanding the risks and applying appropriate safeguards.

1. Separate Your Secrets

The single most impactful thing you can do: don't store API keys where agents can read them.

  • Use a dedicated secrets manager (1Password, Bitwarden, or your OS keychain) instead of .env files or shell profiles
  • If you must use environment variables, load them only in the specific terminal sessions that need them—not globally
  • Never store healthcare-related credentials on the same machine where you run experimental AI tools
  • Treat your ~/.ssh directory as crown jewels—restrict agent access to it

2. Use Permission Systems

Choose agents that ask before acting, and check that the asking is switched on. Claude Code's Manual mode requires approval before file edits or command execution, but since version 2.1.283 new interactive sessions start in auto mode, where a classifier model reviews actions instead of you. Press Shift+Tab or launch with claude --permission-mode manual to get the prompts back. Its hook system can block specific actions entirely—for example, preventing any write to files matching *.env or *credentials*.

NanoClaw's containerization approach goes further: the agent physically cannot access files outside its designated directory. Even if it's compromised, the blast radius is contained.

3. Verify Before You Install

When a coding agent recommends a package or dependency, verify it exists and is legitimate before installing. Check the package registry directly. Look at download counts and publication dates. A package with 3 downloads published yesterday is not the same as one with 3 million downloads maintained for years.

4. Review Before You Push

"I ship code I don't read" makes for a great headline. It's a terrible security practice. Before any code leaves your machine:

  • Run git diff and actually read what changed
  • Search for hardcoded secrets: grep -r "API_KEY\|SECRET\|PASSWORD\|TOKEN" .
  • Check that no .env files are staged for commit
  • Look for unfamiliar package imports you didn't request

5. Understand What You're Connecting

Before giving any agent access to a messaging platform, cloud service, or database, ask yourself: what's the worst thing that could happen if this agent were compromised? If the answer involves patient data, financial records, or privileged system access—reconsider, or add additional safeguards.

The Medical Analogy

Think of coding agents like procedural sedation. Incredibly useful. High benefit-to-risk ratio when done properly. But you wouldn't administer it without monitoring, without knowing the reversal agent, without having thought through what could go wrong. The same discipline applies here: use the tool, but maintain situational awareness.

Conclusion: Match Access to the Task

Agents make a different promise from chatbots: they can pursue work across steps and systems on your behalf. OpenClaw's broad integrations, Hermes's memory, and newer personal assistants show why the category is appealing. They also show why the permissions and approval points matter as much as the quality of the model's answers.

A useful first experiment has a small goal, limited data, and a reviewable result. Clinical use needs a much stronger path: approved data handling, bounded access, source and action logs, and a qualified person responsible for consequential decisions.

As products change, revisit what each agent can read, remember, send, and modify. A familiar interface does not mean the underlying authority has stayed the same.

A question to ask before every connection

What can this agent reach, what can it change without my approval, and how will I inspect or stop its work? Narrow those boundaries before giving it a larger task.

April 2026 Update: The Exploits Keep Landing

Claude Code deny-rule bypass. After Claude Code's source was leaked, security firm Adversa found that once a command chain passed 50 subcommands, Claude Code stopped enforcing its deny rules and asked the user for approval instead of blocking the command. No CVE was assigned to our knowledge, and Adversa said it appeared fixed in version 2.1.90. The lesson: deny rules are a guardrail, not a hard boundary, and an approval prompt only protects you if someone reads it. [Source]

GitHub Copilot CVE-2025-53773 (CVSS 9.6). Hidden prompt injection in a pull-request description enabled remote code execution on machines running Copilot. Anyone in a health-system engineering org using Copilot to review PRs should confirm their deployment is patched. This is prompt injection graduating from “concerning research” to “near-10 CVSS on a production tool used by millions.”

Meta internal AI data leak (March 2026). A Meta employee posted a routine technical question on an internal forum; an AI assistant's response exposed sensitive user and company data to internal engineers. Two hours later, a major security alert fired. The incident is a template for what will happen when internal help-desk AIs at health systems hit the same failure mode.

Prompt injection is still the #1 AI vulnerability. OWASP keeps prompt injection at LLM01 (its 2026 LLM Top 10, published in August, kept it first), and enterprise readiness remains low—only 34.7% of organizations have deployed dedicated defenses. For clinicians: if your health system is piloting agent-based workflows, ask explicitly what prompt-injection controls are in place, and where they've been tested. [OWASP LLM01]

MCP Dev Summit North America (NYC, ~1,200 attendees) and MCP maintainer expansion (April 8) signal that the agent-tool integration standard is maturing fast—see How AI Talks to Your Tools for the clinician-facing explainer.

June 2026 Update: New Defenses, New Attacks, and a Government Recall

OpenAI Lockdown Mode (June 4). OpenAI extended Lockdown Mode, first offered on enterprise plans in February, to personal and self-serve Business accounts. It limits the features that send data out—live browsing, Deep Research, agent mode, file downloads—so a prompt injection has fewer ways to exfiltrate what it finds. It does not change memory, and OpenAI says it “does not prevent prompt injections from appearing in the content ChatGPT processes.” Worth knowing the category of defense exists, and what it leaves uncovered.

Revisiting the Doctronic red-team. Later commentary (example) returned to the March demonstration described above. In Mindgard’s write-up, the public chatbot, fed a fake guideline, produced a visit summary (a SOAP note) recommending a tripled OxyContin dose, the kind of note that is sent to the physician before a consult; Mindgard also reports that the summary was stored and carried into later chats as medical history. Doctronic and Utah say controlled substances cannot be renewed in the pilot. No patient harm has been reported. The takeaway for any AI-drafted note or order: the human review step is a real control, not a formality.

OWASP, June 11. A new OWASP report reaffirmed that prompt injection still drives the majority of agentic-AI security failures in production, reporting a sharp year-over-year rise in prompt-injection attempts. [Source]

A model-level safety story you can point to. Anthropic publicly released Claude Fable 5 and the cybersecurity-focused Mythos 5 on June 9 with added cyber/bio/chem safeguards—then, on June 12, a US government export-control directive forced Anthropic to suspend both models over a demonstrated jailbreak technique (Anthropic disagreed that a narrow jailbreak warranted a recall). The controls were lifted June 30, Fable 5 returned globally on July 1, and it has since been succeeded by Fable 5.1; Mythos remains limited to vetted organizations. A vivid real-world case study in frontier-model risk—and in how fast the controls, and the politics, around these models can change.
